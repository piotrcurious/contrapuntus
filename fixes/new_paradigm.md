### Towards a neurocognitive theory of music: from sound waves to musical experience

I think you are pointing at a fundamental problem with conventional music theory: it describes the organisation of musical objects much better than it explains how those objects become perceptible in the first place.

Traditional theory tends to start with notes, intervals, scales, chords, rhythms and harmonic functions. These are already highly processed abstractions. It often takes for granted the process that produces them: how a biological auditory system extracts periodicity from a changing pressure field, distinguishes one sound source from another, detects a frequency transition, establishes a temporal reference, and develops expectations.

Consequently, there is a strange division of labour:

* Physics explains the vibrations.

* Music theory explains relationships between idealised notes.

* Neuroscience explains many of the mechanisms of hearing.

* Musicians develop practical knowledge connecting these domains, frequently through experiments that they cannot easily articulate in theoretical language.

The missing piece is a sufficiently integrated theory.

I would construct one around a different starting proposition:

Music is the deliberate organisation of changes, relationships, and predictions in an adaptive auditory system. Notes and chords are useful perceptual categories, not the fundamental units of musical experience.

I will call the proposed framework Relational Auditory Dynamics (RAD). It is a synthesis and an extension of existing research, not a claim that a new neural mechanism has already been discovered.

Let's build it from the bottom up.

### 1\. What the auditory system actually detects

### 1.1 The ear is not a microphone connected to an FFT

![Patient's Guide to the Normal Ear | Otolaryngology⁠ — Head & Neck Surgery | Stanford Medicine](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/leHa0_QOkGmyK17OGLHg4Rd80as7rMHUrMYzoisu-FiC04jdTFQ6nbbDaprOE2cQ6QjhqiLczTJ5lhrC4gGapkWbs0tcLnPAWN38pP1SKFKV-cAm0-n4lp1ORdEYbx2sPWQ-aluD0dLvQBj5HJwlLpyddYa5jUdwJGAO5X8kEBBkp4w7b75I8lpLjLqJG_xy?purpose=fullsize)

A microphone produces an electrical signal proportional to fluctuations in sound pressure. The auditory system does something considerably more interesting.

Sound causes vibrations in the eardrum, which are transferred through the ossicles to the cochlear fluids. This generates travelling mechanical waves along the basilar membrane.

Different positions along the membrane respond preferentially to different frequencies:

* High frequencies produce maximal responses near the cochlear base.

* Low frequencies produce maximal responses nearer its apex.

* Intermediate frequencies are represented between them.

This is called tonotopic organisation.

But the cochlea is not merely a passive filter bank.

There are two important types of hair cell:

Outer hair cells (OHCs) actively amplify mechanical vibrations. Through their prestin-dependent electromotility, they change length in response to electrical signals, feeding mechanical energy back into the cochlea. This sharpens frequency selectivity and increases sensitivity.

Inner hair cells (IHCs) perform most of the sensory transmission to the auditory nerve. Mechanical displacement of their stereocilia changes their membrane potential, which controls neurotransmitter release at specialised ribbon synapses.

Approximately 90–95% of auditory afferent fibres receive their input from inner hair cells. PubMed Central (PMC) +2

Already, there is an important departure from conventional musical abstractions: the ear is performing nonlinear, adaptive mechanical processing before the signal reaches the central nervous system.

### 1.2 Hair cells encode movement, not a static musical quantity

Your distinction between stationary potential and differential signals is relevant here.

At rest, some of the mechanotransduction channels in a hair cell are already open. Displacement of the hair bundle in one direction increases channel opening and depolarises the cell; displacement in the opposite direction reduces channel opening and hyperpolarises it.

Consequently, a sinusoidal mechanical stimulus can generate a corresponding oscillating receptor potential.

The inner hair cell is not simply measuring the average displacement of its hair bundle. It preserves information about the direction and temporal structure of the movement.

However, there is a frequency-dependent limitation.

At relatively low frequencies, the receptor potential can follow the acoustic waveform. At higher frequencies, the membrane's electrical properties increasingly smooth out individual cycles, producing a more sustained depolarisation. The auditory nerve then carries a combination of temporal and firing-rate information. PubMed Central (PMC) +2

This is important because the auditory system uses at least two complementary ways of representing pitch.

Place coding: the spatial pattern of activity across frequency-selective cochlear channels.

Temporal coding: the timing of auditory-nerve spikes relative to the periodic acoustic waveform, known as phase locking.

The relative contributions of these mechanisms to pitch perception remain an active research question. Their combination is more plausible than treating either as a complete explanation. PMC +1

### 1.3 Your retinal analogy: where it works

![The Evil That Men Do | American Scientist](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/elHj1egVt9JpWl9_gSUSzL6D1YuvPyen55WvRGye-xlpCZC9W4FIMW4vbJNrHW_cGA8XJM2251s-Q3uXNfHYCe5HnYYtBWoDQBTbA4Zqwabnfi7dcKMPtaKgAP1MI5Jqdi2fEUzzLC1b2VeVA6WTKb7d5Ioz0hXZbeJUmFN11F4V065yJ-HoAXJIWxeA4a6R?purpose=fullsize)

In the retina, photoreceptors respond to illumination, but much of the information that leaves the retina is organised around differences in illumination.

Horizontal and bipolar cells contribute to spatial antagonism. Retinal ganglion cells have receptive fields with centre-surround organisation. This makes changes in local contrast especially informative.

The auditory system also contains circuits that respond strongly to changes rather than simply reflecting the absolute magnitude of stimulation.

For example, consider a small frequency increase in a pure tone.

The peak of the cochlear excitation pattern shifts along the basilar membrane. Some previously more strongly activated neural channels decrease their response, while neighbouring channels increase theirs.

The nervous system can compare those changes.

This is particularly interesting because correlated noise in neighbouring neural channels can partially cancel when their responses are compared. The difference can become detectable even when the absolute change in either channel is extremely small.

Oxenham discusses precisely this mechanism in his review of auditory coding. PMC

This provides a strong physiological basis for your intuition.

But there is an important qualification:

The auditory system is not simply a collection of retinal-style opponent cells.

The cochlea supplies a frequency-organised representation; adaptation and contrast processing occur across several levels of the auditory pathway. The mechanisms are distributed and considerably more diverse than a simple subtraction operation.

### 1.4 Adaptation: why music is especially interesting

One of the most important properties of auditory neurons is adaptation.

Present a sustained sound to an auditory neuron. Its firing rate often rises sharply at onset and then decreases towards a lower sustained response.

The system adapts to recurring and statistically predictable stimulation. Sensitivity changes according to the recent acoustic environment.

This gives the auditory system several advantages:

* It avoids exhausting its dynamic range on persistent stimulation.

* It becomes more sensitive to new events.

* It can distinguish meaningful changes against a background.

* It adjusts to the statistical characteristics of its acoustic environment.

This is not restricted to the auditory nerve. Adaptation occurs at multiple stages of auditory processing. PMC

There is a subtle consequence for music.

A note played continuously for ten seconds is not perceptually equivalent to ten seconds of ten independently articulated notes, even when their nominal pitch and total duration are identical.

The difference involves attacks, releases, interruptions, modulation, adaptation and the listener's expectations.

A musical note is therefore not simply a frequency held for a duration.

It is an event with a temporal history.

### 2\. The missing unit of conventional music theory: the auditory event

Traditional notation presents a note as something like:

\\text{C4},\\quad \\text{quarter note},\\quad mf

This is a convenient symbolic description, but it omits much of what actually determines the perception.

Consider three different instruments playing the same C4:

* A piano produces a sharp attack, evolving harmonic spectrum and decaying amplitude.

* A flute produces a comparatively smooth onset and sustained spectral structure.

* A bowed string can begin with friction noise, followed by a changing harmonic spectrum and vibrato.

The fundamental frequency can be identical.

The auditory experience is not.

More importantly, the brain has to determine which components belong together before it can establish the identity of the resulting sound.

This is the domain of auditory scene analysis.

The auditory system uses several grouping principles:

| Mechanism           | What it contributes                                                      |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Common onset        | Components starting together are likely to belong to one source.         |
| Harmonicity         | Components related to a common fundamental tend to fuse.                 |
| Common modulation   | Components changing together tend to be grouped.                         |
| Frequency proximity | Nearby components are more likely to interact perceptually.              |
| Spatial coherence   | Components arriving from a common direction can be grouped.              |
| Continuity          | A sound source can remain perceptually continuous through interruptions. |

These are not arbitrary aesthetic preferences. They are useful ways of reconstructing sound sources from overlapping acoustic signals.

The common-onset principle is particularly important. The brain frequently establishes an auditory object before assigning it qualities such as pitch and timbre. PubMed Central (PMC) +2

This suggests a substantial revision of the traditional musical vocabulary:

The fundamental object of music should be an evolving auditory event, not an idealised note.

A minimal event description could contain:

E_i =
\\left\\{
t_i,\\,
A_i(t),\\,
S_i(f,t),\\,
P_i(t),\\,
\\Phi_i(t),\\,

\\right\\}

Where:

* : amplitude envelope;

* : evolving spectral distribution;

* : pitch or periodicity estimate;

* : phase and modulation information;

* : spatial information.

This is not intended as a literal neural data structure. It is a better theoretical description of what a listener must infer.

It also explains why timbre cannot be treated as merely decorative. Timbre participates in the creation, separation and identification of musical events.

### 3\. Demystifying pitch, intervals and scales

### 3.1 Pitch is an inference

A pure sine wave has one frequency.

A musical instrument normally produces a complex waveform containing multiple spectral components.

For a harmonic sound, these components approximately follow:

f_n=n f_0

where is the fundamental and is the harmonic number.

For example, a sound with components at:

110,\\ 220,\\ 330,\\ 440,\\ 550,\\ 660\\ \\mathrm{Hz}

can be perceived as having a fundamental pitch of 110 Hz.

Even when the 110 Hz component is physically absent, the auditory system can still perceive the corresponding fundamental. This is the missing-fundamental phenomenon.

Pitch therefore cannot simply be identified with the strongest frequency component.

The brain can infer periodicity from the timing structure, recognise relationships between harmonics, and integrate both temporal and spatial-frequency information. PubMed Central (PMC) +1

This has an important consequence:

A note is not a frequency. It is a perceptual interpretation of a sound, normally involving an estimate of pitch and its temporal continuity.

This also explains why certain percussion sounds have no clear pitch, why some inharmonic instruments produce ambiguous pitches, and why the same sound can yield different perceptual interpretations depending on its spectrum and context.

### 3.2 Why the musical scale is logarithmic

Here, traditional music theory gets something fundamentally right.

Human pitch perception is approximately organised by frequency ratios rather than fixed differences in hertz.

An interval is naturally represented as:

I=12\\log_2\\left(\\frac{f_2}{f_1}\\right)

where is measured in semitones.

A doubling of frequency gives:

12\\log_2(2)=12

That is one octave.

The logarithmic relationship is a useful representation of how pitch distances behave perceptually.

But the familiar twelve equally spaced semitones are not dictated by the cochlea.

They are one particular tuning system:

f_n=f_{\\mathrm{ref}}2^{n/12}

Equal temperament divides the octave into twelve identical logarithmic steps.

Compare two traditional harmonic relationships:

| Interval      | Frequency ratio | Pure interval | 12-TET      |
| ------------- | --------------- | ------------- | ----------- |
| Major third   | 5:4             | 386.31 cents  | 400 cents   |
| Perfect fifth | 3:2             | 701.96 cents  | 700 cents   |
| Octave        | 2:1             | 1,200 cents   | 1,200 cents |

The major third differs by nearly 14 cents between the pure and equal-tempered versions.

Why does this work?

Because equal temperament is an engineering compromise. It allows instruments to operate in different keys without having to change their tuning systems continually.

The mistake is to confuse the tuning convention with the biological mechanism of hearing.

A musician can hear and use intervals outside the twelve-tone system. Microtonal music is not an exception to auditory physiology.

It simply explores a different way of partitioning the perceptual pitch space.

### 3.3 Why simple ratios matter, but not magically

The Pythagorean tradition was not entirely misguided.

Simple frequency ratios often produce harmonic relationships with overlapping partials and comparatively low levels of sensory roughness.

For example, two harmonic sounds in a 3:2 relationship can share some spectral components or support a coherent common harmonic structure.

But consonance is not simply a function of the arithmetic complexity of two numbers.

There are several interacting phenomena:

* Beating and roughness: nearby spectral components can interfere within auditory filters, creating amplitude fluctuations.

* Harmonicity: components fitting a common harmonic series tend to produce perceptual fusion.

* Spectral interaction: timbre and register determine which components interfere and how strongly.

* Cultural learning: exposure changes how listeners categorise and evaluate musical intervals.

* Sensorimotor and emotional associations: familiar vocal, bodily and musical patterns influence the experience.

These explanations are not interchangeable.

A critical review of consonance and dissonance research finds that there is no single accepted theory that explains the phenomenon completely. It examines acoustic roughness, vocal similarity, psychocultural and sensorimotor explanations together. PubMed +1

A useful experiment is to play the same interval using different timbres.

A combination that sounds relatively smooth on a sine-wave synthesiser may sound distinctly rough on a bright organ with strong upper harmonics.

The interval ratio has not changed.

The spectral interaction has.

This is why a theory based exclusively on note ratios is insufficient.

### 4\. Harmony is two different phenomena disguised by one vocabulary

One of the most persistent confusions in music theory is the tendency to mix two distinct problems:

* Why do simultaneous sounds combine into a particular perceptual experience?

* Why does a sequence of sounds make a listener expect another particular sound?

The first is primarily an acoustic and perceptual problem. The second is a temporal, cognitive and cultural problem.

Traditional harmony combines them under terms such as consonance, dissonance, tension, resolution and harmonic function.

Let's separate them.

### 4.1 A chord is a simultaneous auditory construction

Suppose an instrument plays C, E and G.

Several things happen simultaneously:

* The cochlea receives multiple frequency components.

* Their respective partials interact within frequency-selective channels.

* Some partials reinforce or coincide.

* Others generate beating or roughness.

* The auditory system attempts to group related components into coherent sound objects.

* Depending on the instrument and voicing, the notes may fuse into a relatively unified sonority or remain perceptually distinct.

The result is not simply three independent frequencies added together.

The auditory system constructs a perceptual organisation from their combined behaviour.

This explains why orchestration can radically change the character of a chord without changing its nominal notes.

It also explains the difference between a close-position chord, a widely spaced chord and a chord distributed across several instruments.

Their symbolic descriptions might be identical, but their auditory organisation is not.

### 4.2 Harmonic function is learned expectation

Now consider a dominant seventh chord resolving to a tonic.

Traditional theory describes this as a dominant resolving to a tonic, often explaining the movement through tendencies of individual notes.

But where does the sensation of resolution actually come from?

Several processes contribute:

* The listener has learned a statistical grammar of tonal music.

* Particular pitch relationships have acquired different degrees of contextual stability.

* The preceding harmony changes the probabilities assigned to subsequent events.

* Voice-leading patterns create expectations about melodic movement.

* Rhythmic placement, duration and accentuation determine when a resolution is anticipated.

A dominant seventh chord in a musical tradition where it regularly resolves to the tonic establishes a strong expectation.

The same chord presented without this contextual learning does not carry exactly the same functional meaning.

The dominant does not possess a physical force pulling it towards the tonic.

Rather, the listener has developed a system in which that transition has become highly meaningful.

Research on auditory expectation describes this through probabilistic learning of musical sequences and the expectations that emerge from experience. PubMed +2

This distinction is particularly important:

Acoustic dissonance and harmonic tension are not the same thing.

A chord can have substantial sensory roughness but no expectation of resolution. Another can be acoustically smooth but feel harmonically unresolved because of its position in a familiar musical sequence.

A composer can manipulate these mechanisms independently.

For example, a smooth-sounding chord can be placed in an unexpected harmonic position. Conversely, a harsh cluster can be introduced as an entirely expected and stable element of a composition.

That is a much more powerful way of thinking about harmony.

### 4.3 What is a musical key?

A key can be understood as a learned and contextually updated hierarchy of pitch expectations.

Certain pitches become more probable, familiar or structurally important than others.

The tonic is a particularly important reference point. Other pitches acquire different degrees of stability in relation to it.

This can emerge from exposure to recurring musical patterns and from the hierarchical organisation of pitch perception.

A 2025 review by Samuel Mehr proposes two complementary systems supporting human musicality: tonal perception, which establishes pitch hierarchies, and metrical perception, which organises temporal hierarchies. PubMed +1

However, we should not confuse the capacity to learn musical hierarchies with the particular hierarchy found in European tonal music.

Different cultures organise pitches and musical time differently. Even within Western music, modal, minimalist, experimental and other traditions can weaken or abandon conventional tonal hierarchies.

A major scale does not contain happiness. A minor scale does not contain sadness.

Their emotional associations involve more complicated combinations of contour, register, tempo, timbre, cultural learning and musical context.

### 5\. Rhythm: the auditory system is also a temporal prediction machine

![Frontiers | Time Perception for Musical Rhythms: Sensorimotor Perspectives on Entrainment, Simulation, and Prediction](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/s5w8g5376cB4h0VXirmkKYBsr2Tmi4yBHafKtX_8gOzYbkwe81OMkRHDVzlP4TyWMX_C7rn99pwTLd1kQVoOoyUYSNqp_8fmSmWGMTv_6gnADnBBTP-Ew_ks5e5jhZBvHc0rHcUy35Zs3uso2WaldeM1y_3zkMgSbicjbNsZCYQoZ1YvT9Ed7b0psBz-X5GF?purpose=fullsize)

Rhythm is another area where conventional notation conceals an important physiological problem.

A musical score can tell us that a note begins at beat three.

It cannot, by itself, explain why the listener perceives that point as beat three.

The brain has to infer a temporal organisation.

Consider hearing:

\\text{kick — snare — kick — snare}

The listener doesn't simply register four separate events.

A temporal regularity begins to emerge. The listener develops an expectation about when the next event will occur.

This process involves several mechanisms:

* detection of acoustic onsets;

* extraction of temporal regularity;

* anticipation of future events;

* neural synchronisation with periodic patterns;

* interaction with motor systems involved in movement and timing.

The motor system is especially interesting. People can often perceive a beat without actually moving, but accurate rhythmic prediction and synchronisation involve sensorimotor processes. PubMed +1

### 5.1 Why a beat is not just a division of time

Imagine a percussionist playing a perfectly regular sequence of clicks.

Then imagine another percussionist playing an identical sequence but introducing small deviations in the timing and strength of particular attacks.

The nominal tempo might remain 120 BPM.

The second performance can feel completely different.

Why?

Because the listener doesn't perceive only the time between successive events.

The listener develops an internal temporal reference and evaluates incoming events relative to it.

A syncopated note can be particularly effective because it occurs where the listener predicts something different, or where an expected event is omitted.

The resulting perception involves both the actual event and the prediction that preceded it.

There is also an interesting interaction between auditory stimulation and ongoing neural dynamics. The 2025 paper Musical Neurodynamics proposes that resonance and synchronisation across brain–body dynamics help explain musical anticipation, stability, groove and other musical experiences. This is an active theoretical direction, not a final consensus. Nature +1

### 5.2 Meter as a hierarchy of temporal expectations

A 4/4 rhythm is not simply four equal durations repeated.

The listener can develop different expectations for:

* the main beat;

* subdivisions;

* the bar;

* phrase boundaries;

* larger formal repetitions.

These operate over different timescales.

The resulting structure gives composers enormous flexibility.

An event can be:

* acoustically prominent but metrically weak;

* acoustically quiet but metrically important;

* rhythmically predictable but harmonically surprising;

* metrically surprising but harmonically predictable.

This is why rhythm and harmony can generate independent dimensions of expectation.

A coherent musical theory should represent these separately.

### 6\. A proposed unifying theory: Relational Auditory Dynamics (RAD)

We can now construct a more formal framework.

Its central principle is:

A musical composition is an organised trajectory through the perceptual state of an adaptive auditory system.

Instead of beginning with musical notation, begin with an incoming acoustic signal:

x(t)

The auditory system transforms this signal into a collection of frequency-selective, time-dependent responses.

We can represent the output of the cochlear channels as:

C_k(t)

where identifies a frequency-selective channel.

For each channel, we can estimate:

* its evolving energy;

* its envelope;

* its periodicity;

* its transient structure;

* its relationship to neighbouring channels.

Then we introduce five interconnected levels.

### The five layers of RAD

Layer 1

Sensory differentiation

Acoustic changes, frequency contrast, adaptation, onset and offset detection.

Layer 2

Auditory object formation

Harmonicity, common modulation, onset synchronisation, source separation and timbre.

Layer 3

Relational organisation

Pitch, intervals, melodic trajectories, spectral fusion and simultaneous relationships.

Layer 4

Temporal organisation

Beat, meter, synchronisation, accents, rhythmic grouping and motor anticipation.

Layer 5

Learned expectations

Tonal grammar, stylistic expectations, memory, surprise, tension and musical meaning.

These are analytical levels, not five independent anatomical structures. They interact continuously, and the auditory pathway has extensive recurrent connections. Recent research increasingly challenges the idea of a simple one-way progression from ear to cortex. Nature +1

Let's give this framework some mathematics.

### 6.1 Layer 1: differential sensory representation

For each cochlear channel, let:

E_k(t)

represent its estimated envelope.

We can model an adaptive reference level:

B_k(t)

which represents the recent background activity in that channel.

A simple differential measure is:

D_k(t)=\\log(E_k(t)+\\epsilon)-B_k(t)

The derivative:

N_k(t)=\\frac{dD_k(t)}{dt}

describes how quickly the channel departs from its adaptive background.

This captures an important distinction:

A slowly changing background and a sudden increase in energy can have quite different neural and perceptual consequences, even when their instantaneous amplitudes are identical.

We can also introduce spectral contrast:

S_k(t)=
\\log(E_k(t)+\\epsilon)
-
\\sum_j w_{kj}\\log(E_j(t)+\\epsilon)

where the weights describe the influence of neighbouring frequency channels.

This is a computational abstraction of local spectral comparison, not a claim that the brain literally calculates this equation.

It gives us a useful representation of a sound as a changing pattern rather than a list of stationary frequencies.

### 6.2 Layer 2: perceptual grouping

Now we need to determine which frequency components belong together.

For two components and , define a conceptual grouping score:

G_{ij} =
aO_{ij}
+bH_{ij}
+cM_{ij}
+dL_{ij}

Where:

* : similarity of onset timing;

* : harmonic relationship;

* : similarity of amplitude or frequency modulation;

* : spatial coherence.

The coefficients are not universal constants. They depend on acoustic conditions, listening conditions and the task.

A high grouping score means that the components are more likely to be interpreted as parts of the same auditory object.

This explains a number of musical phenomena within one model.

For instance:

* A chord played by one instrument may fuse into one sound.

* The same chord distributed between independent instruments may become perceptually separated.

* Vibrato can help a melodic line stand out.

* A percussion transient can distinguish the onset of an instrument from its sustained harmonic body.

Importantly, this model also provides a place for polyphony, counterpoint and complex orchestration. They become problems of controlling perceptual grouping and segregation.

We can describe pitch salience using a combination of spectral and temporal evidence:

* weights individual harmonics;

* and determine the relative contributions.

Again, this is a conceptual model, not a definitive description of the actual neural circuitry.

The important thing is that pitch becomes an estimate supported by multiple kinds of evidence.

We can then represent the pitch of a musical event in logarithmic coordinates:

p_i=12\\log_2\\left(\\frac{f_i}{f_{\\mathrm{ref}}}\\right)

and an interval as:

\\Delta p=p_{i+1}-p_i

But this is only one aspect of a melodic relationship.

Two identical intervals played in different registers, by different instruments, with different envelopes and durations need not produce identical experiences.

Traditional interval theory describes one coordinate of a much larger perceptual space.

### 6.4 Layer 4: extracting the beat

Suppose our extracted musical events have onset times:

T=\\{t_1,t_2,\\ldots,t_n\\}

and event saliences:

A=\\{a_1,a_2,\\ldots,a_n\\}

We can estimate candidate beat periods by searching for regularities in the weighted onset sequence.

A simplified beat-coherence function is:

\\sum_i a_i
\\exp\\left\[
-\\frac{
d_{\\mathrm{circ}}(t_i,\\phi,\\tau)^2
}{2\\sigma^2}
\\right\]

where:

* is a candidate beat period;

* is its phase;

* is the distance from an event to the nearest predicted beat;

* determines the timing tolerance.

This produces candidate rhythmic references.

The auditory system need not choose the same one as another listener. Half-time and double-time interpretations, for instance, can both be viable.

The model makes an important distinction between the physical timing of events and their perceived metrical organisation.

### 6.5 Layer 5: expectation and musical syntax

Finally, the listener develops expectations.

Let represent the perceptual description of event , containing its pitch, timbre, onset, duration, modulation and other relevant attributes.

A learned musical model estimates:

P(z_{i+1},\\Delta t_i\\mid z_1,\\ldots,z_i,\\mathcal{C})

where represents musical context and learned stylistic experience.

A simple measure of surprise is:

I_i=-\\log P(z_i\\mid z_1,\\ldots,z_{i-1},\\mathcal C)

An unexpected chord, rhythmic interruption or change of timbre can therefore produce a different degree of surprise depending on the preceding musical history.

But surprise alone is not tension.

Tension also depends on the listener's confidence in a prediction, sensory roughness, unresolved temporal or melodic patterns, physiological activation and the possibility of a forthcoming event.

This is where expectation-based models and neural-dynamical models become complementary research directions rather than competing explanations that have to be reduced to one formula. PubMed +2

### 7.1 Why musicians know things that their theory cannot explain

There is an interesting epistemological problem here.

A skilled musician can often recognise that a note is slightly out of tune without being able to quantify the deviation.

They can hear that a chord is poorly balanced without being able to identify precisely which partials are interfering.

They can anticipate a rhythmic accent without consciously calculating the subdivisions of a measure.

They can improvise over an unusual progression because their perceptual and motor systems have learned relationships that are not necessarily represented as explicit theoretical knowledge.

This is procedural learning.

The musician's brain builds internal models through repeated interaction with sound and instruments.

Theoretical knowledge and practical auditory knowledge are therefore different representations of musical structure.

A score can describe a note as a C sharp. The performer has a much richer internal representation involving its expected sound, relationship to other pitches, physical production, tuning, timbre and place in the musical sequence.

The practical musician has learned to operate on perceptual relationships.

The traditional theorist has often learned to operate on symbolic relationships.

Both are useful, but they are not equivalent.

### 8\. What happens when we apply RAD to actual musical situations?

Let's consider some examples.

### Example A: A sustained organ chord

The conventional theory describes a collection of notes.

RAD asks a series of different questions:

* How do the organ's spectral components interact within cochlear channels?

* How strongly do the components fuse into a single auditory object?

* How does the sustained sound adapt perceptually?

* Are there amplitude or phase relationships producing beating?

* Does the listener hear a stable sonority or anticipate a change?

* Does the harmonic context support or undermine the apparent stability?

The duration of the chord becomes a genuinely musical variable rather than an empty interval between two notated events.

A sustained chord can change perceptually without changing its nominal pitches. Its interaction with the listener's adaptive auditory system is changing.

### Example B: A distorted electric guitar

A distorted guitar may produce a large number of additional spectral components.

Traditional theory might describe the result simply as a chord played with distortion.

RAD can explain why distortion changes the identity of the chord:

* Additional partials alter the spectral distribution.

* Intermodulation can generate components not present in the original signal.

* Stronger high-frequency energy can change roughness and sharpness.

* Adjacent notes can interfere differently.

* The original chord may become harder to segregate into individual pitches.

In other words, distortion can alter the perceptual topology of a musical object.

This is not merely an aesthetic effect added after the notes have been chosen.

It changes the acoustic information from which those notes are inferred.

### Example C: A syncopated bass line

Suppose a bass player plays a relatively quiet note shortly before the expected downbeat.

The note can become highly salient even though its acoustic amplitude is modest.

Why?

Because it arrives at an important position relative to the listener's temporal prediction.

The event is interpreted through the relationship between its actual timing and the anticipated beat.

This also explains why a musician can produce a strong groove using very small changes in timing and accentuation.

The musical importance of an event is not proportional to its physical amplitude.

### Example D: Microtonal music

Suppose we use an instrument with 19 or 31 equal divisions of the octave rather than 12.

Traditional theory might require a new scale, new interval names and a new harmonic vocabulary.

RAD does not.

The sensory mechanisms remain essentially the same. The listener still extracts periodicity, compares pitch relationships, detects spectral interactions and learns the new system's regularities.

What changes is the learned categorisation and expectation structure.

This is a major advantage of the approach: it does not assume that Western twelve-tone equal temperament is the natural endpoint of human auditory evolution.

### 9\. How to turn the framework into an experimentally testable musical theory

I would insist on one additional requirement: a theory of music should make measurable predictions.

Otherwise, we merely replace the old magical mathematical machinery with new neuroscientific terminology.

RAD could be developed into a computational and experimental framework with five independent types of measurement.

Proposed experimental programme

1\. Differential sensitivity

Change one frequency, transient, envelope or partial at a time. Measure the perceptual threshold and how it varies with the surrounding spectrum.

Test: What information is actually necessary to detect a musical change?

2\. Auditory grouping

Manipulate onset synchronisation, harmonicity, vibrato and modulation coherence independently.

Test: Under what conditions do components become one perceived object or separate streams?

3\. Relational pitch

Compare pure tones, harmonic complexes, missing fundamentals, inharmonic spectra and microtonal intervals.

Test: How does the brain estimate pitch and distinguish one interval from another?

4\. Temporal prediction

Manipulate rhythmic deviations while preserving average tempo, duration and event count.

Test: How do timing expectations, surprise and sensorimotor synchronisation interact?

5\. Musical learning

Expose listeners to unfamiliar tonal and rhythmic grammars, then measure how their expectations change.

Test: Which perceptual properties are relatively stable, and which are acquired through musical experience?

A particularly revealing experimental design would independently manipulate the following:

* frequency ratios;

* harmonic content;

* amplitude envelope;

* onset synchronisation;

* rhythmic placement;

* preceding musical context.

This would help separate the physical properties of sounds from the perceptual and learned properties that we normally bundle into musical labels.

It would also help avoid a serious methodological problem in neuroscience: inferring a specific cognitive process merely because a particular brain response occurs during a musical task. Andrew Goldman's 2025 analysis of neuroscience in music research discusses precisely these problems of reverse inference and the need for better-defined musical behaviours. University of California Press

### 10\. What this theory changes about composition and musical instrument design

One of the most interesting consequences is that composition becomes a problem of designing perceptual dynamics.

Instead of asking only:

Which chord should follow this chord?

We can ask:

* Which auditory features should become more salient?

* Which components should fuse into one sound?

* Which components should separate?

* How much spectral change is needed to create a perceptible transition?

* How long should an event persist before adaptation changes its prominence?

* When should the listener's temporal prediction be confirmed or violated?

* How much uncertainty should be maintained?

* Which aspects of the sound should remain stable to preserve continuity?

These questions can lead to compositional techniques that conventional harmony does not adequately describe.

For example, imagine a composition in which the nominal pitch remains unchanged for several minutes, but the sound gradually moves through different harmonic structures, spectral envelopes, temporal modulations and spatial configurations.

Traditional analysis might call it a drone.

RAD would recognise a potentially elaborate sequence of transformations in perceptual identity, even though the fundamental pitch remains stationary.

Likewise, a composition might use a conventional chord progression but manipulate the timbral and temporal dimensions so aggressively that harmonic function becomes only one element among several.

This provides a systematic way to analyse experimental electronic music, ambient music, industrial music, microtonal music and complex polyrhythmic compositions without forcing them into the categories of European tonal harmony.

There is also an engineering consequence.

A synthesiser or musical instrument should not necessarily be designed around the assumption that twelve discrete pitch buttons, a fixed set of chord shapes and conventional rhythmic subdivisions are the most natural interfaces.

An instrument could expose independent controls for:

* fundamental periodicity;

* harmonic distribution;

* spectral motion;

* modulation frequency;

* onset and decay;

* inter-channel phase relationships;

* rhythmic prediction and displacement.

A musician could then explore the perceptual consequences of changing these variables directly.

This is a much more interesting approach to instrument design than simply constructing another keyboard with more notes.

### 11\. What is established, what is hypothetical, and what remains unresolved?

We should be careful not to make RAD sound more scientifically established than it is.

One particularly important development is the increasing recognition that the auditory system is not simply a feedforward chain of processing stages. A 2026 review by Lohse, Willmore and King describes the auditory pathway as an integrated, recurrent cortical–subcortical network. This makes a rigid separation between peripheral sensation, brainstem processing and higher musical cognition increasingly difficult to defend. Nature +1

That is a useful direction for a future theory: the system should be modelled as an adaptive, interacting network rather than a sequence of independent mathematical transformations.

### 12\. The fundamental philosophical correction

I would go further than simply adding neuroscience to music theory.

I would reverse the direction in which we normally construct the explanation.

Traditional music theory tends to begin with a mathematical structure and then attempts to explain why humans perceive it as musical.

The proposed approach begins with the properties and limitations of human perception and then asks why certain mathematical structures have become useful in musical practice.

The difference is substantial.

A harmonic ratio is not inherently beautiful. It is a relationship between oscillations that interacts with the auditory system in particular ways.

An interval is not a little mathematical object travelling from one note to another. It is a relationship constructed by the listener between perceptual estimates.

A chord is not merely a stack of frequencies. It is an attempt by the auditory system to organise simultaneous acoustic components into a coherent experience.

A rhythm is not merely a collection of durations. It is a temporal structure interpreted through adaptation, anticipation and movement.

A musical key is not a universal gravitational field. It is a learned system of relative pitch expectations.

And musical tension is not a physical force. It emerges through the interaction of sensory interference, prediction, memory, temporal organisation and bodily response.

Music is not fundamentally about notes. It is about how an adaptive nervous system organises acoustic change into relationships, continuity, expectation and possibility.

That, I think, is the most useful foundation for a genuinely neurocognitive music theory.

### One direction I would pursue further

There is a potentially powerful computational consequence of all this.

We could build a perceptual music analyser and experimental synthesiser that does not start with MIDI notes or conventional music notation.

Its primary representation would be a time-frequency field, with adjustable models of:

* cochlear filtering;

* adaptation and differential sensitivity;

* harmonic and temporal pitch extraction;

* auditory object grouping;

* roughness and harmonicity;

* temporal prediction and entrainment.

It could then display what happens when a musician changes a particular acoustic parameter, alongside the predicted perceptual consequences.

That would turn the theory into something experimentally useful: instead of arguing whether a particular chord is consonant, we could investigate which properties of its sound, presentation and learned context make it so.

The eventual objective should be a theory capable of explaining conventional harmony without being confined to it—and capable of describing music that conventional harmony has no adequate vocabulary for.
